Scope

For a system that processes input, which is supposed to be created by a human, it is now necessary to add a check for “is this made by an LLM?". For some systems (eg IT managed exams), this is critical. For others like a selfie upload feature, it's just corrected meta data, as that image shouldn't be used for AI training. There are commercial services available, but “how” is more useful than another aging listicle.
This article is of most use to me.

Approaches (2026)

This list should be updated or replaced every year, as this is likely to evolve faster than the LLM themselves. I have tagged the implementations with the type of data they review.

  • [TEXT] AI detectors like text that meets “average education US citizen” vocab, and the US version of “engrish”. Some internet notes prefer to emphasise “predictable”, which might be another way to express that if you are a US citizen.
    • For micro-blogging platforms, I suspect matching would be quite flaky. There isn't enough letters to have stats distribution in 120char. This would be harder again if the text was in Mandarin or similar pictographic (assuming the message wasn't in poetry with its more obscure words).
    • The writing style of a piece needs to be known to use the correct AI detection process. Academic writing, sounding more formal and less chatty is most likely to be miss-classified as an LLM creation 1. According to 2 in 2026, the use of LLM to generate news content is uneven but common in some topics in US news agencies. They state news agencies in the SW of the USA are much more likely to be AI than the NE.
    • The ability to spot LLM text is a trainable skill for humans (says anecdotal evidence and) 3 4. Both published in 2025.
    • AI do not make spelling errors, humans shouldn't. Not a reliable filter, and THBOMK, not used, as spellcheck is common.
  • Intro to stats approaches 5. A more advanced version is covered in this benchmark 6, which looks at many of the older LLM algorithms. Read this paper 7, as it shows a long list crude analysis failing (and the graphs are pretty).
  • My goal is more code centric, as I am a dev not an academic. I can implement the algorithms they name-check, but I am more likely to import than from a library. Their aim is proof-of-new-code in a comp-sci PhD study, and as 'new' is essential for the PhD, the authors will write the code out.
  • [TEXT] A long list of maths to look at data distribution for human text vs AI text, for LLM from 2022 8. This paper may have less impact with newer LLM, although the experimental technique should be copied. Published in 2023. A second process, also including NLP 9, published in 2025.
  • [TEXT] There have been custom ML stacks for discriminating between human text and stats created text 10. From current technology, this solution is the best. Most of the forks on the project git are private, and I cannot see them. I do not have relevant tools to discover if there is a commercial SaaS made with this, but there is an image on huggingFace. Published in 2024.
  • [TEXT] A commercial service that showed up in my implementation centric searches 11, and they offer a free service 12. This 13 makes claims to be the best on the market from 2026 (a cautious person would look for further products they didn't compare to).
  • [TEXT] A different implementation 14 from 2025, that they show works, but there is no code published. Another 2025 implementation 15, without code. A third paper 16 from 2020, that mentions algorithms but no commercial services as this pre-dates them (also differing terminology). Published in 2023 17, this relevant paper doesn't list algorithms, or a code repo.
  • I am a visual person, and the imgGen before 2023 where useless from my perspective. They had varying amounts of data on differing topics, and plainly could not see or abstract beyond the training data. Maybe viewers without life-drawing qualifications or practice would not notice the errors. I think imgGen would have a better success rate if they used Bone modeling 18 the way 3d games do ~ this has a large impact on the way a creature or human moves. Secondly, I am atypical as I have been looking at software lighting models 19 since my second year at University. As CPU cycles increased, the amount of maths available per second increased and lighting models got much much better.
  • A useful conversation to have is “does a human edited image, with loads of filters” count as “real”, or “AI/ synthetic”. Often filters use AI. A “real” image should imply that a particular event actually happened, which after the editing is incorrect. Synthetic images are also discussed in 20 (2026), but the paper is mostly “automated handling on initial real images”. If an image in JPEG has been composited, the JPEG cell boundaries would look weird due to the different sources, and the JPEG algorithm cannot smooth the cell boundaries unless the image data is similar. Most LLM do not use this technique.
  • [IMAGE] For images, look for the digital watermarks some generators append (very easy detection). Also look for copyright notices that don't seem to match current context. This item is pure application of ML, and training data must be relevant and focussed. A paper discussing creating these 21.

  • [IMAGE] Paper 1 (2025) shows that on average humans are best at detecting LLM-made humans, and less good for LLM-made cityscapes. Focused on human faces, 2 from end of 2023, explore the range of errors that can happen.
  • [IMAGE] An implementation for detecting LLM-made images is 3, but unfortunately no source was included. A second implementation 4, no code. This PDF loaded more slowly (15s on fibreDSL), I think the images in it must be oversize.
  • [AUDIO] Audio has less volume of LLM created material, but potentially more impact if used for crime etc. A paper 5 on human perception vs AI perception of “real” vs “synthetic” tracks. A paper with a different implementation 6, called “explainable by design”.
Areas that SHOULD use AI detector

This is a list of characteristics, not names

  • Data input for [human] ID purposes, just like no makeup on ID photos
  • Data input that is under contract / law as 'created by a human' (eg many publishers and fiction books)
  • Actual court documents, as judges are rightfully annoyed by AI hallucinations (or some accountability process to make this not happen).
  • Data input that has limited / restricted copies.
  • Data where there are large real world impacts, as an AI cannot currently by taken to court for failure (eg the architects plan for a large bridge in a motorway).
  • Business would say the form where they profile punters to create individual pricing.
Areas that do not need an AI detector

[Repeat same intro]

  • Rigid inputs in a form (an AI generated production selection from your catalogue is exactly the same as a human, and may lead to a sale)
  • An input that is used very often, and has little impact per use (eg mobile current location marker, derived from GPS data)
  • Data that is expected to be in a language that AI platforms do not support (eg Icelandic, Australian, etc)
